Project overview

HydroFleet was highly successful, winning the Excellence in Drone Applications Award at the Farm Robotics Challenge for its innovative coordinated aerial design and robust mechatronic execution.

By implementing variable-rate irrigation (VRI) driven by HydroFleet maps, agricultural operations can increase crop yields by up to 15% while simultaneously cutting water and energy expenses by 25% to 40%.

The underlying technology is the subject of a newly filed Utility Patent Application with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office. As a core mechanical engineer on the team, I designed, simulated and fabricated the entire Soil Probing Module (SPM): a novel electromechanical payload that lets a drone lower, anchor and physically drill a capacitive moisture probe into soil to a depth of over 5 inches, capturing direct ground-truth measurements without landing.

Mechanical and mechatronic design: the Soil Probing Module

Developing a soil probing mechanism capable of generating the high linear forces needed to penetrate compacted clay, while staying within a strict 600 g payload weight budget, was the core mechanical challenge of the project.

Key hardware innovations

Single-motor dual linear-rotational actuation

Early prototypes used two independent motors to manage linear feed and rotation separately. I engineered a highly optimized single-motor gear drive system around a 25 kg/cm continuous servo motor. By routing the output through a custom-machined 1:1:2 gear ratio train, the rotation of the 3D-printed helical auger is perfectly synchronized with its downward linear descent along a fixed trapezoidal lead screw (Tr8x8). This forces the auger thread to cut into the soil at exactly its natural pitch rate, drastically reducing the torque required for penetration and saving significant motor weight.

High-torque anchoring mechanism

Reacting the rotational torque of drilling in compacted clay would otherwise spin the suspended drone out of control. To prevent this, I developed an active anchoring system using three high-torque coreless micro-servos (MEUS Racing, 8.5 kg/cm) spaced at 120° intervals around the base plate. They deploy curved, tapered circular claws into the soil, while static nails on the underside of the pocketed aluminium base plate prevent rotation.

Rotating electrical slip ring

To cut cleanly, the capacitive sensor body must rotate in unison with the auger. This created a major electrical failure mode: wires would twist and snap. I integrated a 6-wire electrical slip ring directly on top of the rotating carriage, maintaining noise-free, continuous data connectivity between the rotating capacitive sensor and the stationary Arduino Nano microcontroller.

Astable sensor circuit and CAN bus networking

To read moisture, we used the capacitive probe from a General Tools DSMM500. I discarded its bulky 300 g housing and built a custom signal-conditioning circuit using an LMC555 CMOS timer configured in astable mode. The probe acts as a dynamic capacitor: as volumetric water content increases, capacitance shifts and alters the output pulse frequency. That frequency is converted into an analog voltage read by the Arduino Nano.

To keep electromagnetic noise from the adjacent servos and brushless drone motors from corrupting the sensor readings over the 2-meter slung cable, we implemented a robust CAN bus network: an MCP2515 CAN transceiver shield on the Arduino Nano talks to a Waveshare USB-CAN-A converter on the flight companion PC.

Field evaluation and testing

We validated the fully integrated HydroFleet platform through extensive field testing under realistic agricultural conditions at Powisset Farm in Dover, MA, a 109-acre organic Community Supported Agriculture (CSA) vegetable farm.

  • 97% insertion success rate: across repeated trials in highly compacted, organic clay soils, the active circular anchoring claws completely prevented the module from lifting or twisting, giving a 97% success rate.
  • Repeatable depth tracking: precision depth tracking of ±2 mm was achieved by combining Tr8x8 lead-screw rotation counters with live ultrasonic distance tracking between the upper and lower plates.
  • Zero data degradation: sensor VWC readings recorded via the autonomous single-motor astable oscillator showed a correlation of R² = 0.947 against manual, hand-inserted controls, verifying that high-frequency vibrations from the auger drive did not corrupt the capacitive dielectric measurements.

Design story and swarm evolution

Achieving a reliable, robust and lightweight system required multiple architectural pivots. These were the major engineering challenges and the design adaptations that answered them.

CHALLENGE 01

Severe slung-payload oscillations

Initial tests suspending the 570 g payload from a single point caused severe rotational transfer, turning the drone in flight. I resolved this by designing a 3-point bridle line of high-tensile braided cord coupled to a low-friction marine fishing swivel, completely isolating payload rotation from the airframe dynamics.

CHALLENGE 02

Excessive stepper motor weight

Early prototypes used heavy dual stepper motors for carriage translation and auger rotation. To hit our 600 g budget, I replaced both with a single continuous-rotation servo motor and engineered a custom 3D-printed 1:1:2 ratio gear system to drive both axes simultaneously.

CHALLENGE 03

Insufficient anchoring force

When drilling into compacted clay, the vertical resistance forced the sampler upward, lifting it off the ground. We upgraded passive spikes and 9 g servos to three active 8.5 kg/cm coreless servos with tapered circular claws, which slide deep into clay perpendicular to the normal force of the auger.

CHALLENGE 04

High electromagnetic noise

High-frequency noise from the drone's ESCs and brushless motors corrupted the analog sensor lines. We isolated the signals with a local CAN bus network: an MCP2515 CAN controller on the sampler's Arduino Nano transmits differential signals over the 2-meter line.
