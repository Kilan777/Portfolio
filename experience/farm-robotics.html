HydroFleet

A drone swarm that maps soil moisture by drilling a probe into the field, built with MIT for the Farm Robotics Challenge.

2025–Present Olin College × MIT Farm Robotics Challenge
Excellence in Drone Applications award Utility patent filed, 2026

A drone lowers the Soil Probing Module and drills the probe into the field.

HydroFleet is a multi-drone system developed with a research team at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). Each drone lowers a slung probe module into the field and drills a capacitive moisture sensor into the soil, and the fleet turns those readings into a soil moisture map for variable-rate irrigation. I designed, simulated and fabricated the Soil Probing Module, the payload that does the drilling.

Overview

HydroFleet won the Excellence in Drone Applications Award at the Farm Robotics Challenge. Variable-rate irrigation (VRI) driven by HydroFleet maps can raise crop yields by up to 15% and cut water and energy costs by 25% to 40%. A scientific paper on the system is in progress.

The technology is the subject of a utility patent application filed with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office. My part was the Soil Probing Module (SPM), the electromechanical payload that lets a drone lower, anchor and drill a capacitive moisture probe more than 5 inches into the soil, taking a ground-truth measurement without landing.

Design

The main mechanical problem was generating enough linear force to penetrate compacted clay while staying inside a 600 g payload budget.

Single-motor drive for feed and rotation

Early prototypes used two motors, one for linear feed and one for rotation. I replaced them with a single 25 kg/cm continuous servo driving a 1:1:2 gear train, so the 3D-printed helical auger rotates in step with its descent along a fixed Tr8x8 trapezoidal lead screw. The auger thread then cuts into the soil at its own pitch rate, which lowers the torque needed to penetrate and saves motor weight.

Anchoring

Without anchoring, the drilling torque in compacted clay would spin the suspended module and the drone above it. Three coreless micro-servos (MEUS Racing, 8.5 kg/cm) at 120° around the base plate deploy curved, tapered claws into the soil, and static nails on the underside of the aluminium base plate stop the module from rotating.

Slip ring

The capacitive sensor body rotates with the auger, so its wires would twist and snap after a few cycles. A 6-wire slip ring on top of the rotating carriage carries the sensor signal to the stationary Arduino Nano.

Sensor circuit and CAN bus

The moisture sensor is the capacitive probe from a General Tools DSMM500, with its 300 g housing removed. I built a signal-conditioning circuit around an LMC555 CMOS timer in astable mode: the probe acts as the timing capacitor, so as volumetric water content rises the output pulse frequency shifts. That frequency is converted to an analog voltage read by the Arduino Nano.

Noise from the servos and the drone's brushless motors corrupted analog readings over the 2-metre slung cable, so the sampler talks to the flight companion PC over CAN bus: an MCP2515 transceiver shield on the Arduino Nano and a Waveshare USB-CAN-A converter on the PC.

Build

The finished module is built from pocketed aluminium plates on the lead screw, 3D-printed gears and carbon fibre tubes, and weighs 570 g as a payload.

Testing

We tested the integrated system at Powisset Farm in Dover, MA, a 109-acre organic CSA vegetable farm.

  • 97% insertion success rate across repeated trials in compacted clay soil; the anchoring claws kept the module from lifting or twisting.
  • ±2 mm depth tracking, from lead-screw rotation counts combined with an ultrasonic distance reading between the upper and lower plates.
  • R² = 0.947 between VWC readings from drone-inserted probes and hand-inserted controls, so vibration from the auger drive did not corrupt the capacitive measurement.

What I learned

The design went through several changes between the first prototype and the field version, and each one came from something that failed in testing.

Slung-payload oscillation

Suspending the 570 g payload from a single point transferred its rotation to the drone in flight. A 3-point bridle of braided cord on a low-friction marine fishing swivel isolates payload rotation from the airframe.

Stepper motor weight

Early prototypes used two stepper motors for carriage feed and auger rotation. To hit the 600 g budget I replaced both with one continuous-rotation servo and a 3D-printed 1:1:2 gear train that drives both axes.

Anchoring force

Drilling into compacted clay pushed the sampler upward and lifted it off the ground. Passive spikes and 9 g servos were replaced with three 8.5 kg/cm coreless servos driving tapered claws that slide into the clay perpendicular to the auger's reaction force.

Electromagnetic noise

Switching noise from the ESCs and brushless motors corrupted the analog sensor lines. Moving the link to a CAN bus, with an MCP2515 controller on the sampler's Arduino Nano, sends differential signals over the 2-metre line instead.
